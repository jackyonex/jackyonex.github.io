Why the photos look different

Camera distance changes the proportions. In the close selfie, my nose is closer to the camera than my ears, so it looks larger. From farther away, that difference in depth matters less, and my face looks more natural. The street photos show the same effect: nearby buildings look much larger when I stand close, while the view from farther away looks flatter. Zoom changes the size of the image without changing those proportions. In the dolly zoom, I stepped back and zoomed in to keep the car about the same size. The house behind it was much farther away, so stepping back changed its distance only a little; the zoom made it look larger.

Part 2: Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Collection

Each glass plate holds three black-and-white exposures of one scene, taken through blue, green, and red filters. They appear from top to bottom in B, G, R order. I split the plate into those three channels, shift G and R to match B, then stack them into an RGB image.

Step 1: Splitting the plate

I read each plate as a grayscale image and divide its height into three equal parts. Integer division drops one or two leftover rows at the bottom. A 1024 × 396 plate, for example, gives three channels of 341 × 396 pixels.

Before comparing channels, I convert the pixel values to floats. The image initially stores each pixel as uint8, an integer from 0 to 255. That type cannot represent a negative result: 3 − 5 would wrap around to 254 instead of −2. Floats let me calculate those differences correctly. I also divide the values by 255 to put them in the range [0, 1].

Step 2: Scoring an alignment with L2

I score each possible shift by measuring the L2 distance between the shifted channel and the blue channel:

L2(a, b) = sqrt( sum over all pixels of (a - b)^2 )

For each pixel, I subtract one channel value from the other, square the difference, add up the squares, and take the square root. A smaller score means the channels match more closely.

I use np.roll to shift a channel. It keeps the image size fixed by wrapping pixels that leave one edge around to the opposite edge. Those pixels would give misleading scores, as would the dark plate borders, which differ between channels. I therefore score only the middle of each image, leaving out 10% on every side. On a small channel, the vertical margin is about 34 rows, more than the largest shift I search, so wrapped pixels stay outside the scored area.

Step 3: Single-scale exhaustive search

For the green channel, I try every vertical and horizontal shift from −20 to 20 pixels and score each result against blue. That makes 41 × 41 = 1681 positions. I keep the shift with the lowest L2 score, then repeat the search for red. Blue stays fixed as the reference.

I first tried a ±15 search window. The results for 01728v and 01598v still showed red and cyan fringes, so I tested ±20, ±25, and ±30. At ±25 and ±30, 01269v became visibly misaligned. The ±20 window worked best across the 12 small images, so that is the one I used.

Step 4: Image pyramid for the large images

On the large plates, each color channel is about 3238 × 3800 pixels, and the shifts are much bigger than on the small images. A wide search at full resolution would exceed the assignment's target of under one minute per image. I used an image pyramid instead: start with a small copy to estimate the shift, then refine it at larger sizes.

Shrinking by half. Before keeping every other row and column, I blur the image so a narrow detail is less likely to disappear between sampled pixels. In each direction, the blur uses (neighbor + 2 × current pixel + other neighbor) / 4. This is a convolution with the kernel [1, 2, 1] / 4, applied vertically and then horizontally. The weights add to one, so the blur keeps the overall brightness unchanged.

Coarse-to-fine search. When the image is small enough, I run the same single-scale search used above. Otherwise, I halve both images and search at that smaller size first. I double the returned offset to bring it back to the current scale, then check a ±2 neighborhood for a better match. I repeat this as I return to full resolution. The wide search happens only on the smallest copy, where it costs about as much as it does for a small JPEG.
